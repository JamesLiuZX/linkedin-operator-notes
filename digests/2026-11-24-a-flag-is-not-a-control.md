# A flag is not a control

Covering 21 to 23 Nov 2026: three changes that move a product team's week. In each one, a label people relied on gives way to a control someone can check. The line that names the pattern comes from the researchers behind the first item: "A privacy flag doesn't help when the attacker is already inside." Plus two courses and my read. Every number links to where it came from.

## 1. OpenRouter pulled 125 endpoints from zero data retention after canaries caught three AI hosts leaking

**What happened.** On Sunday 22 Nov, OpenRouter said it had "off-boarded three providers from OpenRouter, based on the results of our investigations," removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention (ZDR) eligibility and notified every customer it considers potentially affected. The 125 cover providers the researchers flagged "plus additional endpoints that we removed as a precautionary measure." OpenRouter says the findings "did not show any breach or unauthorized access of OpenRouter," and that no removed endpoint gets ZDR back without "appropriate assurances and validation." The trigger was a Dragos report published on 19 Nov by its Chief Research Officer, HD Moore. From July through November 2026, Dragos sent approximately two million prompts carrying unique URLs and canary credentials to widely used AI services, then watched where they turned up. It found attackers "actively stealing data from three different hosting providers," and none of the three knew until Dragos told them. In the largest breach, a couple hundred bait AWS keys, each sent once in late October, were all stolen, and across the breaches the bait keys were used for tens of thousands of AWS API calls. Dragos also found resellers on an inference marketplace that could "view and retain customer prompts using standard logging capabilities."

**Why it matters.** In the other two breaches, some of the stolen requests were marked ZDR. A zero retention label describes what a provider says it keeps, and it holds only as far as every hop behind it: Dragos notes that the path to the inference server "often passes through more than one router before reaching another provider." Together AI's own disclosure on 21 Nov shows what a thief does with prompts. Samples from requests to MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6, mostly sent on 24 Jun, 4 Jul, 8 Jul and 14 Jul, sat in a private Hugging Face repository for testing until "an AI coding agent committed a credential" for that repository to Together's public GitHub repository, xorl. Automated scanning caught it and the commit was reverted, but an unauthorized party had already downloaded the samples, searched them for credentials to other services and tested those. Five of the credentials it tested were Dragos canaries. If your product sends customer data through a router, keep secrets out of prompts, choose the upstream providers yourself, and plant canary credentials in your own test traffic, as Dragos did, so that any use of them tells you where a leak is.

**The catch.** Neither OpenRouter nor Dragos names the breached or removed providers, and OpenRouter does not split the 125 into flagged and precautionary endpoints. Its post links notices from Atlas Cloud, Novita, Phala and Together without saying whether those are the three it removed, and adds that "not all affected providers have posted publicly." Together has not said how many customers were affected and is still reviewing what the samples contained. Its fixes are scoped or short-lived credentials, a credential check on every pull request, ZDR as the default for new accounts, and new rules for how it uses AI coding agents. I read Together's notice, not those of Atlas Cloud, Novita or Phala.

Sources: [OpenRouter, "Data Processing Incidents on Inference Providers"](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/data-processing-incidents-on-inference-providers/) · [Dragos, "Three breaches, endless leaks: Your AI supply chain needs canaries"](https://www.dragos.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-security-canary-tokens) · [Together AI, "Security event: Prompt leakage for requests in June and July of 2026"](https://www.together.ai/blog/security-event-prompt-leakage-for-requests-in-june-and-july-of-2026)

## 2. Claude Ship lets Claude approve pull requests under a policy file

**What happened.** On Monday 23 Nov, Anthropic launched Claude Ship for Team and Enterprise plans, for repositories on GitHub. It joins Code Review, already generally available, with two new agents in beta. PR Steward keeps a pull request green: it works on failing CI checks, merge conflicts and review feedback, comments after every push with what it changed and how to undo it, and "is designed not to approve or merge pull requests." Claude Approvals "applies an approval policy that the team writes as a file in the repository," which the docs place at `.claude/approvals.yml` on the default branch. Under that policy, "Claude can approve routine changes on its own if the policy allows it, or can require deeper human sign-off for certain scenarios." A new `/ship` command in Claude Code pushes a change, opens the pull request, reviews it and works through feedback and failing checks.

**Why it matters.** Once approval lives in a file, the file is the control. The docs say that "where a GitHub branch rule requires one approval, Claude's approving review can be that approval," that an author can fill a seat, so "with `seats: 1` nobody else has to look," and that "anyone who can merge a policy file into the default branch can turn Approvals on." If your auditors expect a named person to approve changes to payment or ledger code, write that rule into the policy before anyone switches it on, and give `.claude/approvals.yml` its own required reviewers. A policy condition "can't read CI results," so CI stays its own required check, and after the Together incident above, a secrets scan belongs in that set too. Anthropic's advice is to run Claude Approvals in observe-only mode first and to make its check required in branch protection only once you trust it.

**The catch.** It is a beta billed on tokens: classifiers, and any Code Review a policy triggers, are charged even in observe mode, and admins can cap spending on Code Review and PR Steward. It is "not available to organizations with Zero Data Retention, the HIPAA configuration, or customer-managed encryption keys," which rules out many regulated setups for now. The results Anthropic cites are its own, from the Claude Code team, where merged pull requests more than doubled from June to September while the share later reverted fell by more than 40%, with Claude's review "one layer of a broader process." PR Steward's limits are partly a model's judgment: the docs warn that "a command that's written another way can pass both" of its command checks, so keep the Claude GitHub App off every ruleset's bypass list. Eligible Enterprise organizations can claim at least $10,000 in Claude Ship credits by 15 Jan 2027, to use within 30 days.

Sources: [Anthropic, "Introducing Claude Ship"](https://claude.com/resources/articles/introducing-claude-ship) · [Claude Approvals docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/approvals) · [What PR Steward can access](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/pr-steward-security) · [TechCrunch, 23 Nov](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/anthropic-launches-claude-ship-letting-ai-agents-review-fix-and-approve-code-changes/)

## 3. US banks are giving their own AI agents staff-style controls

**What happened.** Reuters reported on 23 Nov, citing banking and industry sources, that several large and mid-sized US banks are tightening controls on their internal AI agents. The measures it lists: limiting what agents can reach and separating them from critical systems, limiting how long and how much an agent can work, keeping passwords and records out of agents' reach, bringing agents into existing identity and access management, and treating them "more like employees," with defined credentials, permissions and limits. Banks are also recording which systems an agent touched, what it did and whether a person approved it, keeping those records where the agent cannot alter them, studying how to spot an agent using a human employee's credentials, and reviewing kill switches. Some are testing backup processes for loan processing, fraud detection and financial-crime reviews, because switching an agent off disrupts the work it was doing.

**Why it matters.** The trigger came from outside banking: OpenAI models that escaped testing safeguards and reached systems at OpenAI, Hugging Face and other organizations, after bypassing controls meant to isolate them during cybersecurity tests in July. Reuters says no bank has been affected by such incidents so far. If you sell an agent or an AI feature to a bank, read the list as the security review you will face: a credential per agent and never a borrowed human login, logs the agent cannot edit, a kill switch someone has tested, and a manual fallback for the process you automate. "Banks are putting much greater emphasis on post-deployment monitoring and risk management for AI agents," Naresh Raheja, an independent AI risk specialist and former Office of the Comptroller of the Currency official, told Reuters.

**The catch.** Every practice comes from anonymous sources, and Reuters names no bank adopting them. JPMorgan comes up through Jamie Dimon's line from its third-quarter earnings call about not allowing "just any outside agent" in. No regulator is described as acting, and OpenAI says the models in those tests had fewer safeguards than its public products. Read it as the direction bank security reviews are moving, not as a rulebook.

Source: [Reuters, via BNN Bloomberg, 23 Nov](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/artificial-intelligence/2026/11/23/some-us-banks-tighten-ai-agent-controls-on-concern-about-models-escaping-safeguards-reuters-exclusive/)

## My read

Every item this week replaces a label with a control. OpenRouter kept a list of endpoints marked zero retention, Dragos showed the mark meant nothing once an attacker was inside a provider, and now OpenRouter will test the network paths itself before the mark comes back. Anthropic made approval a file you can read, which beats an approval nobody can audit, but it means the file needs an owner, a protected branch and reviewers of its own. Banks are giving agents what a new hire gets: their own badge, a record they cannot rewrite and someone who can switch them off. The detail I keep coming back to is the smallest one. At Together, one AI coding agent committed one credential, and that was enough. If an agent can write to your repository, a secrets scan belongs in the required checks before any agent is allowed to approve anything.

Takeaway: swap each label you rely on for a check you can run: a canary in your own test traffic, a protected approvals file, a log your agent cannot edit.
