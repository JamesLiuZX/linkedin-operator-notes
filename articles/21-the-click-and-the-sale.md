# AI creative wins the click and loses the sale

A study by researchers at Columbia, Harvard, and Carnegie Mellon, analyzing over 500M ad impressions, found AI-generated ads achieved a 0.76% click-through rate against 0.65% for human-created ads. On the headline metric, AI creative won. The same study, measuring further down the funnel, found conversion rates for AI-generated ads dropped 8% on high-consideration purchases, widening to 14% on purchases over $500. On the metric that turns into revenue, AI creative lost, and lost more the more expensive the thing being sold.

Both numbers describe the same 500M impressions. Neither is wrong. They are answers to 2 different questions a single "AI ads outperform" headline collapses into 1, and the 0.11-point CTR gap, 0.76% against 0.65%, is a small enough number on its own that the study's real finding is not "AI wins," it is "AI wins narrowly on 1 metric and loses meaningfully on another, depending entirely on what is being sold."

## Two decisions wearing one metric

A click and a purchase are not the same customer decision, and the gap between them widens exactly where the study found the conversion drop: expensive, high-consideration items. Clicking a scroll-stopping ad costs a viewer nothing and takes half a second of curiosity. Buying a $500-plus item after clicking requires trusting the product enough to commit real money to it, and that trust is where AI-generated creative's advantage reverses. The same qualities that make a piece of creative unusually good at earning a click, novelty, a slightly uncanny polish, an unfamiliar visual pattern the eye has not learned to ignore yet, are not obviously the qualities that build the kind of confidence a $500 purchase decision runs on.

On lower-priced products, the study found the AI-generated creative reaching full conversion parity with human-made ads. The conversion gap is not evidence that AI creative underperforms in general. It is evidence that the gap opens specifically as consideration and price rise, exactly the range where a viewer's decision shifts from "does this stop my scroll" to "do I trust this enough to pay for it," and where the 2 questions a single CTR number is quietly averaging together stop having the same answer.

## Where the same technology is winning outright

Meta's Advantage+ Shopping campaigns, which test 150-plus creative combinations automatically per campaign, delivered 32% lower cost per acquisition and a 15 to 20% CTR lift over manually configured campaigns. Smartly.io's analysis of 6.7M ad placements found AI-driven creative rotation cut frequency-related performance decay, the point where a repeated ad's CTR starts dropping because the same audience has seen it too many times, by 38.4%, holding ads above baseline CTR for an average of 19.3 days against 7.1 days for a static, non-rotated campaign.

Both of those wins sit in the same territory the conversion-parity finding describes: higher-volume, lower-consideration decisions, where more creative variety and faster iteration are a direct, uncomplicated advantage with no countervailing trust cost. Nothing about the 8-to-14% conversion gap touches this range, because the gap is a finding about high-consideration purchases specifically, not a general tax on anything AI-generated.

## What a single CTR number hides

This is the same shape essay 14 makes about a LinkedIn post's reach number: the metric that is easiest to report is not automatically the metric that reflects what actually happened to the reader, or here, the buyer. A team reporting "AI creative beat our control on CTR" is reporting a true, real number that, on its own, says nothing about whether it also beat the control on the metric that pays the bills. For a low-price, high-volume product, that omission usually does not matter, because the study found parity there. For a $500-plus purchase, the omission is hiding the more important half of the result.

## Why the CTR number is the one that gets reported

CTR is available same-day, sometimes same-hour, straight out of the ad platform's own dashboard. A trustworthy conversion number, especially for a $500-plus purchase with a sales cycle that can run days or weeks, takes longer to settle and requires connecting the ad platform to whatever system records the actual sale. A team under pressure to show a result from a new AI-creative pilot has an easy number ready well before the hard number is, and the easy number is the one that made AI creative look like a clean win in the first place. This is not a claim that anyone involved is being dishonest. It is a claim that the metric with the shorter feedback loop wins the reporting race by default, regardless of which metric actually decided whether the campaign made money.

## What to check before calling it a win

Pull the conversion rate for the same campaign, segmented by price point, before reporting a CTR win as a creative win. If the product sits above roughly $500 and consideration is genuinely high, a real estate service, an enterprise tool, a major appliance, check whether the CTR gain survived contact with an actual purchase decision, or whether it is a click-volume win sitting on top of a conversion loss the top-line number never shows.

Takeaway: a click and a sale are different customer decisions, and AI-generated creative wins 1 of them, 0.76% versus 0.65% CTR, while losing ground on the other, an 8 to 14% conversion drop on purchases over $500. Report both, or the number you report will flatter whichever one you left out. The 32% CPA reduction from Meta's Advantage+ and the 38.4% decay reduction from Smartly.io are both real, both worth using, and both measured in exactly the range, high-volume, lower-consideration, where the conversion gap this piece opens with does not apply.
