# Core writing principles

Non-negotiable for every post, essay, and newsletter issue.

This is not "write better." This is how the best communicators in the world earn attention, keep it, and leave people changed. Steal their moves. Keep your voice.

---

## 1. Never use em dashes

No `—`. Ever. It reads like default AI punctuation.

Use periods, commas, colons, parentheses, or a plain hyphen `-` only when you would in a text to a friend.

## 2. Always have a hook

The first 1-2 lines must earn the next paragraph.

Good hooks: a sharp claim, a contradiction, a scene, a question the reader already half-asks.

Bad hooks: throat-clearing, context dumps, "In today's world…", bio flexes.

**Presenter move:** Open on tension, not credentials. The audience decides in seconds whether you are worth the next minute.

## 3. Write naturally

Sound like a sharp person talking, not a framework deck.

Prefer short sentences. Occasional fragments are fine. Kill corporate filler ("leverage", "unlock", "delve", "robust", "in today's landscape").

If you wouldn't say it out loud to a peer over coffee, rewrite it.

## 4. Always end with the takeaway

Close every piece with a short **Takeaway** section.

Make it concrete enough that a reader can act on it or repeat it. No soft fade-out.

**Presenter move:** People remember the last thing you said more than the middle. End on the line you want repeated in a Slack thread or a hiring conversation.

## 5. Make it shareable

Before shipping, ask:

> Would someone forward this to a friend with "this is so true"?

If the answer is only "it's informative," push harder on the insight. Shareable usually means: a crisp reframe, a useful test, or a line people want to steal.

## Craft from the best presenters (integrate these)

You are not writing vibes. You are building a career asset. Treat every piece like a talk that has to move a sharp room.

### A. One idea. One transformation.

Great talks (Jobs, TED's best, Duarte) do not try to teach twelve things. They change one belief.

Before drafting, fill this in:

> When the reader starts, they believe: _____  
> When they finish, they believe: _____  
> The one sentence that carries that change: _____

If you cannot fill those blanks, you do not have a piece yet. You have notes.

Kill anything that does not serve the transformation. Decoration go in a separate post.

### B. Audience is the hero. You are the guide.

Nancy Duarte's rule: the audience walks in as the hero. You are the mentor with a map, not the star of your own biopic.

- Write so a PM or founder can see *their* product in the story.
- Use "you" when the reader should feel the decision land.
- Use "I" for earned scars and specific observation, not for autobiography.
- Never make the piece about how impressive your resume is. Make it about the decision they have to make on Monday.

Test: if you strip your job title from the piece, does the insight still hold? If not, rewrite.

### C. What is → what could be → new bliss

The structure behind the best speeches:

1. **What is:** name the status quo the reader already feels (shared reality).
2. **What could be:** show the better world if they adopt the idea (gap = tension).
3. **Oscillate:** in the middle, keep flipping between broken reality and the cleaner alternative. Contrast is oxygen.
4. **New bliss:** end with a vivid picture of life after the idea is adopted, plus a clear ask.

Do not dump framework → bullets → goodbye. Make the reader feel the gap before you hand them the ladder.

### D. Make it stick (SUCCESs)

Chip and Dan Heath's filter. Run every draft through it:

| Trait | What it means here | Fail mode |
|-------|--------------------|-----------|
| **Simple** | One core idea you can say in a breath | Kitchen-sink essay |
| **Unexpected** | Break the schema the reader arrived with | Predictable "tips" post |
| **Concrete** | Sensory, specific, testable | Abstract nouns ("trust," "alignment") with no scene |
| **Credible** | Operator detail, honest limits, public-safe proof | Vague authority ("in my experience…") with no teeth |
| **Emotional** | Someone cares about an outcome (money, dignity, fairness) | Pure taxonomy with no human stake |
| **Stories** | A person moves through a situation | Framework with no movie in the reader's head |

You do not need all six at full volume every time. You need enough that the idea lodges.

### E. Open curiosity gaps. Close them on purpose.

The best presenters create a question the brain cannot leave unanswered, then pay it off.

Moves that work in writing:

- Name a paradox ("they won… and never came back").
- Promise a diagnosis, then delay the label one beat while you show the scene.
- End a section on a half-resolved tension that the next section answers.
- Never open a gap you do not close. That is clickbait, not craft.

### F. Contrast is the engine

Humans process contrast faster than explanation.

Build pairs into the piece:

- Spike vs habit  
- Forgivable loss vs feeling hustled  
- More markets vs better markets  
- Decoration vs trust work  
- What leadership celebrates vs what Tuesday reveals  

If a paragraph only asserts, ask: what is it contrasting against?

### G. Concrete over clever

Steve Jobs did not sell "revolutionary synergy." He sold 1,000 songs in your pocket.

In our lane:

- Bad: "Liquidity remains a strategic priority."
- Good: "You can't get out without eating glass."

Prefer: named sources of truth, thirty-second explainers, empty Tuesday, the user who lost fairly. Prefer verbs and objects over abstract nouns.

If a hiring manager at a trading firm cannot picture the bug, rewrite until they can.

### H. Rule of three, then stop

Three trust surfaces. Three tests. Three stages. The brain loves threes. Four starts to feel like a dump.

If you have seven points, you have one essay and six orphans. Cut or sequence.

### I. Rhythm: tension, release, breath

Great delivery has music even on the page.

- Short after long.
- A one-line paragraph after a dense one.
- A plain sentence after a sharp one (so the sharp one lands).
- White space where a speaker would pause.

Read the piece out loud. Where you gasp for air, the reader bounces. Where you naturally punch a word, bold or isolate that line.

### J. Ethos without flexing

Aristotle's three appeals, translated for LinkedIn:

- **Logos:** the test, the framework, the decision rule.
- **Pathos:** the user who felt played; the empty stadium on Tuesday.
- **Ethos:** specific, public-safe scars. "I've watched…" beats "As a leader in…"

Credibility comes from precision and restraint, not from name-dropping the company in every paragraph.

### K. Stealable lines

World-class communicators leave the room with one line people repeat.

Before ship, extract your candidates:

1. The reframe (belief flip)
2. The test (question they can reuse in a meeting)
3. The cohort line (who the work is actually for)

At least one must be tweet-length and true without the surrounding essay.

### L. Show the movie, then name the model

Paul Graham / great essay order:

1. Scene or paradox the reader recognizes  
2. The uncomfortable implication  
3. The named model (only after it is earned)  
4. The practical test  
5. The new picture of the world  

Do not lead with "There are three pillars of…" unless the hook already made the pillars feel inevitable.

## Essay architecture (use this skeleton)

For long pieces (`articles/`), default structure:

1. **Hook:** contradiction or scene (2-4 lines)
2. **Stakes:** why this matters to the reader's product, not to your ego
3. **Call to adventure:** the big idea in one breath
4. **Middle (contrast loops):** 2-4 sections; each section = what is broken → what good looks like → the operator test
5. **The decision rule:** the question that survives contact with a roadmap meeting
6. **New bliss:** life after the idea (specific, not motivational)
7. **Takeaway:** act + repeatable line

Images earn their place only if the caption advances the argument. Pretty stock with no job is decoration. Cut or rewrite the caption.

## Pre-publish checklist

**Mechanics**
- [ ] No em dashes (`—`) anywhere
- [ ] Hook in the first two lines
- [ ] Reads natural out loud
- [ ] Takeaway section at the end
- [ ] At least one line worth quoting or forwarding
- [ ] Public-safe / compliance pass

**Presenter craft**
- [ ] One transformation (start belief → end belief) is clear
- [ ] Audience is the hero; you are the guide
- [ ] What is / what could be contrast shows up more than once
- [ ] Core idea is Simple + Unexpected
- [ ] At least one Concrete scene a stranger can picture
- [ ] Curiosity gaps opened and paid off
- [ ] A decision test the reader can reuse Monday
- [ ] Ending lands on new bliss + the stealable line (not a shrug)
