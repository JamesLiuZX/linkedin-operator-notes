# The tell isn't the face, it's the polish

Arcads, an AI UGC-ad startup founded in 2024, raised a $16M seed round in December 2025 and now serves over 6,000 clients producing roughly 100,000 ad assets a month, drawn from a library of 300-plus AI actors. It is not the most technically polished tool in its category. That is close to the entire reason it works.

One hands-on comparison of 3 leading AI-avatar tools found roughly 70% of HeyGen's outputs needed a re-render or a script fix before they were genuinely ad-ready, against about 40% for MakeUGC and 35% for Arcads. HeyGen is not a worse product by most conventional measures: it supports 175-plus languages with voice cloning and lip-sync, ahead of Synthesia's 140-plus, and produces visibly smoother, more polished output. In this specific comparison, the smoother output was the one that needed more fixing before it worked as an ad.

## The premise UGC is built on

User-generated content advertising works because the format's entire premise is that a stranger, not a studio, is talking to the viewer. The visual grammar of that premise is specific: slightly imperfect framing, a phone camera rather than a rig, a voice that sounds like it is talking to 1 friend rather than performing for an audience. None of that is an accident of low production value. It is the signal that tells a viewer's attention this is not an ad in the traditional sense, which is precisely why it earns attention an obviously-produced ad does not.

An avatar tool optimized to look as polished, as lip-synced, as broadcast-smooth as possible is optimizing directly against that signal. The more the output looks like something a studio made, the more it reads as exactly what UGC's whole design was built to not look like. Audiences on TikTok and Instagram have had roughly this much exposure to AI-generated video by now that a specific visual signature, too-smooth motion, slightly-too-perfect lip sync, gets recognized fast, and recognized content in this format gets scrolled past, not watched, because the recognition itself breaks the premise the format depends on.

## Why the re-render number tells the real story

70% of a tool's outputs needing a fix before they are ad-ready is a production cost, not a taste preference. Every re-render is a delay, a person's time, a reason a campaign ships a week later than planned. Arcads sitting at roughly half that rework rate, 35% against HeyGen's 70%, is not because Arcads is a lesser piece of engineering. It is because Arcads' actor library and defaults were built for direct-response ad performance specifically, which optimizes for the rough, plausible, single-take feel a real UGC creator produces on the first try, rather than for the broadcast-grade polish a demo reel is built to impress a buyer with.

This is a useful general lesson about any tool evaluated by a sales demo rather than by the output it is actually meant to produce. A demo audience rewards polish, because polish is legible in 30 seconds and a persuasive demo has 30 seconds to work with. An advertiser's actual audience, scrolling a feed at speed, rewards the opposite signal for this specific format, and rewards it precisely because it has learned, product by product, what a produced ad looks like and reflexively discounts it.

## Where polish is still the right call

None of this argues that polish is a mistake everywhere generative video gets used. A B2B product explainer, a features-page demo video, an internal training module, all benefit from looking produced, because the viewer is not being asked to mistake the content for a peer's unscripted post. The tell-isn't-the-face problem is specific to formats whose entire value proposition rests on not looking made, which UGC-style advertising is and a corporate explainer is not. Picking a tool by its demo reel, without asking which of these 2 categories the actual deployment falls into, is how a team ends up with the smoother output and the worse ad.

## What "direct-response tuned" actually means

It is not a euphemism for lower quality. It means the tool's defaults, camera framing, cadence, how close the actor stands to the lens, are built to match what a real UGC creator produces on a 1st take with a phone, rather than what a video team produces on a 3rd take with a rig. A 300-actor library built for that target optimizes for range and plausibility across many faces and voices, not for any 1 actor looking as flawless as possible. That is a specific, deliberate engineering choice, not the absence of one, and it is why a tool built this way can serve 6,000 clients a month at a lower rework rate than a tool built to impress in a demo.

## What to check before choosing an avatar tool

Ask what the tool's defaults are actually optimized to produce, not what its most impressive sample looks like. A 35% rework rate against a comparable tool's 70% is the kind of number a sales page will never volunteer, because it is evidence about ad performance, not about how good the technology looks in a pitch meeting.

Line the 3 numbers up: 70% rework for the most language-capable tool, 175-plus languages. 40% for the 2nd tool. 35% for the tool built around a 300-actor library and 6,000 monthly clients, the smallest feature set of the 3 by most conventional comparisons, and the lowest rework rate by a comparison that measured the thing that actually matters for this format.

Takeaway: UGC's entire premise is that it does not look produced, and an avatar tool optimized for visual polish is optimizing against that premise. In 1 documented comparison, the more polished tool needed 70% of its outputs reworked before they were ad-ready. The rougher, direct-response-tuned tool needed half that.
